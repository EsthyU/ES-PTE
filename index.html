    Nine minutes, 80–120 words. Aim 95–110. Nothing else matters if you breach the count.

    How the maximum is earned

    1. Split the nine minutes 2–6–1. Two minutes planning, six writing, one proofing. The proofing minute is where the spelling trait is won, and it is the minute everyone skips.
    2. Answer every bullet explicitly. The prompt carries three requirements. A requirement you addressed inside a subordinate clause counts. A requirement you assumed the reader would infer counts as missed, and content is the trait that gates the rest.
    3. Count at five minutes, not at the end. Discovering you are at 130 words with ninety seconds left is a form penalty you cannot cut your way out of cleanly.
    4. Paragraph it. Four short blocks with a blank line between: purpose, detail, request, close. Organisation is scored separately from grammar, and an unbroken wall of text loses it even when every sentence is correct.
    5. Plant three structures. One subordinate clause, one modal request, one passive. That is the whole grammatical-range evidence and it fits inside the skeleton without adding words.
    6. Pick the register from the addressee, then hold it. Opener, contractions and closer must all agree. "Dear Sir or Madam" closing with "Best" is a register break and it costs the email-convention mark on its own.
    7. Hold one spelling convention. Pearson accepts UK, US, Australian and Canadian spelling, but mixing them inside one response is penalised. You write UK. Stay there: organisation, apologise, programme, travelled.
    8. Never paste a memorised opening unrelated to the prompt. Content scores relevance. A generic paragraph that could sit on any email adds words and no marks, and it pushes you towards the 120 ceiling.
    The three ways this task goes to near-zero

    Under 80 or over 120 words. Answering a different prompt than the one shown. Writing in a register so far from the addressee that appropriacy collapses. All three are avoidable in the planning minute.

    Summarize Written Text

    Ten minutes, 25–50 words. Outside that range scores zero. Target 38–44.

    Time
    10:00
    Range
    25–50
    Target
    38–44
    Sentences
    1 or more
    Traits
    Content, form, grammar, vocabulary

    How the maximum is earned

    1. Find the main claim before you write anything. It is almost never in the first sentence. In a three-paragraph passage it is usually the sentence the second paragraph is arguing against, or the one the third paragraph generalises from.
    2. Content is the gate. Misrepresent the purpose of the passage and the other traits stop mattering. When two readings are possible, take the broader one.
    3. Carry the concession, don't argue it. Passages at this level are nearly always claim-plus-qualification. Putting the qualification in a subordinate clause proves grammatical range and captures content in the same breath.
    4. Land on 38–44 words. 25 and 50 are cliff edges. Anything above 46 and you are one careless clause from zero.
    5. One main clause plus one subordinate clause is the minimum for full grammar credit. A string of simple sentences caps the trait however accurate it is.
    6. Paraphrase the passage's key terms rather than lifting them. Vocabulary scores on appropriate synonym use. Lifting three whole phrases reads as copying and it costs the trait.
    7. Never include an example, a statistic, or a second supporting point. There is no room, and specifics dilute the content score rather than raising it.
    8. Spend the last ninety seconds reading it aloud in your head. Run-on sentences and dropped articles are what a fast writer produces and what a slow reader catches.

    Template A — one complex sentence

    “Although [concession], the passage argues that [main claim], demonstrating that [key evidence or implication].”

    Template B — two sentences

    “[Main claim: subject–verb–object.] This is supported by [evidence one] and [evidence two], suggesting that [implication].”

    Template B is legal in PTE Core and safer if you are prone to run-ons. It is not legal in PTE Academic — don't carry the habit across if you ever sit that test.

    S At ceiling · 5% of study time · maintenance only

    Speaking tasks

    You scored 90. There is nothing above it. Fifteen minutes once a week to hold form: 5 Read Aloud, 5 Repeat Sentence, 2 Describe Image, 2 Respond to a Situation, 5 Answer Short Question.

    Three rules that protect a 90

    Never self-correct — a repeated word costs more fluency than the mispronunciation would have cost. Never pause for three seconds, because the recording closes and takes the rest of your answer with it. Fill the whole response window, every time.

    Read Aloud S + R

    Scored on content, oral fluency and pronunciation. It is the only spoken task that also feeds Reading.

    1. Use the prep seconds to mark phrase groups, not to rehearse the whole text. Find the subject–verb boundary in each clause and breathe there.
    2. Start within a second of the tone. Long dead air before the first word is scored as hesitation even though you have said nothing wrong.
    3. Read every word, including articles and auxiliaries. Content is scored on words actually delivered, and small function words are the ones a fast reader drops.
    4. Even pace beats fast pace. Fluency rewards rhythm and consistent speed, not speed itself.
    5. Do not trail off. The last three words are routinely swallowed and pronunciation is scored across the full response.
    6. If you stumble, carry on. Going back to fix a word converts one pronunciation slip into a fluency penalty across the whole item.

    Repeat Sentence S + L

    The highest-volume spoken item. Content, fluency and pronunciation.

    1. Hold the melody, not the word list. Shadowing the sentence's rhythm and stress recovers more words than trying to memorise them in order.
    2. Begin immediately. Three seconds of silence closes the recording.
    3. Deliver whatever you have in one unbroken flow. A shorter answer said fluently outscores a complete answer said haltingly, because fluency is scored on what you actually produce.
    4. Do not paraphrase or repair the grammar. Content is word-matched against the original.
    5. Do not add filler at the front — no "the sentence was". Every syllable you add is one you are not spending on the sentence.

    Describe Image 25s prep · 40s response

    1. Fill all 40 seconds. Trailing silence is scored exactly as it sounds.
    2. Reach the striking feature by the second sentence. A long generic preamble burns the window that content is scored in.
    3. Name two or three specific values with numbers. Specifics are what the content trait credits; "the chart shows various trends" credits nothing.
    4. Wrong data delivered fluently outscores right data delivered haltingly. Do not stop to check yourself against the image.
    5. End on the overall pattern so the response closes rather than stopping.
    “This [chart type] presents information about [topic]. The most striking feature is that [highest / lowest / biggest change]. In contrast, [second data point]. There is also a noticeable [trend] in [third element]. Overall, the image suggests that [restate headline pattern].”

    Respond to a Situation 20s prep · 40s response

    1. Take the register from the relationship named in the prompt. It always names one — neighbour, manager, colleague, stranger. Appropriacy is scored here in a way it is not in Describe Image.
    2. Address every element of the situation, the same discipline as Write Email. The prompt usually contains two or three obligations.
    3. Acknowledge before you state. One short opening clause that recognises the other person's position reads as appropriacy and costs three seconds.
    4. Offer something concrete. An alternative, a date, a next step. A response that only apologises is thin on content.
    5. Close politely and stop. Padding to fill is more damaging than finishing two seconds early.
    “[Acknowledge appropriately]. [State your position or apology directly.] [One brief reason.] [Concrete alternative or next step.] [Polite close.]”

    Answer Short Question S + L

    One to nine words. Scored correct or incorrect on content alone.

    1. Answer with the bare noun. "A thermometer." Full sentences add nothing and eat the window.
    2. It is a vocabulary check, not a listening check. The recurring fields: everyday objects, occupations, weather, the body, food, time, geography, basic quantities.
    3. Guess in-category rather than staying silent. There is no penalty, and a wrong noun from the right field costs the same as nothing.
    4. Answer in the first second. The window is short and hesitation eats it.
    Microphone

    Set it once during the check and do not move it. Roughly a hand's width from your mouth, angled slightly off-axis so plosives don't clip. Consistent volume matters more than loudness — the scorer is measuring your speech against the room, and a level that drifts mid-response reads as a fluency problem.

    Every recording plays exactly once

    There is no replay control anywhere in this section. Falling behind on Highlight Incorrect Words or Fill in the Blanks is unrecoverable, which makes reading-ahead in the preparation seconds the single highest-value habit in Listening.

    Getting the full mark on each task

    Summarize Spoken Text 8 min · 20–30 words

    Scored on content, form, grammar, vocabulary and spelling. Four of those five are writing traits, which is why this item matters to you more than its Listening weight suggests.

    1. Note in three columns: TOPIC / CLAIM / QUALIFIER. One or two words in each. Do not attempt to transcribe — a full note takes attention the recording will not give back.
    2. Write the scaffold from memory first, before you reconstruct anything: "The speaker explains that ___, noting that ___." That is eight words, leaving 12–22 for content.
    3. Content gates all five traits. Name the wrong topic and grammar, vocabulary, spelling and form all score zero regardless of quality. When the topic is ambiguous, state it in the broadest defensible terms.
    4. Land on 24–28 words. 20 and 30 lose form marks at the edges. Under 5 or over 40 is a zero across all five traits.
    5. One main idea and one qualifier. Nothing else. The example the speaker gave is not the point and there is no room for it.
    6. Reserve the final 90 seconds for mechanics. Subject–verb agreement, tense consistency, one spelling convention throughout, capital at the start, full stop at the end. Grammar, vocabulary and spelling are three separate traits and one careless pass can cost all three.
    7. Never leave it blank and never answer in a phrase. Under five words scores zero on everything.

    MCQ, multiple answers negative marking

    1. Read the question in the seven seconds before the audio starts. It tells you what to listen for, and the difference between listening for a main idea and listening for a detail is the whole item.
    2. Take notes rather than ticking as you listen. The recording almost never presents information in the order the options are printed, so selecting live means re-reading five options mid-audio.
    3. Select only what you can point to in your notes. A wrong selection cancels a correct one, so on a coin-flip the expected value is negative.
    4. Distrust options that reuse the recording's exact words. Lexical overlap is the standard distractor build. The correct option is usually a paraphrase.
    5. Do not assume a fixed number of answers. Two, three and four all occur.
    6. Two confident selections beat four hopeful ones, every time, arithmetically.

    Fill in the Blanks typed · spelling scored

    1. Read the transcript in the preparation seconds and predict each gap's word class before the audio starts. Half the gaps are then decided before you hear anything.
    2. Type as you listen. Waiting until the end means reconstructing from memory with no replay available.
    3. If you miss one, type your best phonetic guess and move on immediately. Stopping to think costs you the next gap, which arrives in seconds.
    4. Spelling is scored here. This is the trap: neither Reading Fill in the Blanks task scores spelling, so the habit does not transfer. A correct word spelled wrong earns nothing.
    5. Fill every gap. No penalty for a wrong word, so a blank is a guaranteed zero where a guess is not.
    6. Use tab, not the mouse, to move between gaps. It keeps your eyes on the transcript.

    Highlight Incorrect Words negative marking

    A transcript on screen, a recording that differs from it in a handful of places. Click the words that differ.

    1. Track the audio with your finger or cursor on the line. Falling behind is the failure mode, and there is no replay to recover with.
    2. Click the instant you hear a mismatch. Pausing to consider whether it was really different costs you the next three words while you deliberate.
    3. Listen for sound, not sense. Substitutions are usually near-homophones or same-class swaps: affect / effect, weather / whether, three / free, quite / quiet. A sentence that still makes sense can still contain a changed word.
    4. Click only what you actually heard differ. A wrong click cancels a correct one, exactly as in MCQ multiple.
    5. Expect five to seven changes. If you finish with twelve clicks you have been guessing and have probably scored zero.
    6. If you lose your place, stop clicking and re-find the line. Blind clicking from a lost position has negative expected value.

    Select Missing Word no penalty

    1. The beep replaces the ending, so the answer completes the argument, not just the grammar. Options are often all grammatically valid.
    2. Listen hardest to the last two sentences. The discourse marker before the beep — so, therefore, however, in other words, instead — tells you whether the ending confirms the line of argument or reverses it.
    3. Track the speaker's direction of travel. Concluding, conceding, qualifying, contrasting. Match the option to the direction.
    4. Always answer. No penalty, and there are only three to five options.

    MCQ, single answer no penalty

    1. Read the stem first: it names what you are listening for. Main cause, which aspect, what the speaker suggests, why the speaker mentions. Those four stems demand four different kinds of listening.
    2. Skim the options in the five seconds before the audio. What they have in common tells you the topic before you hear a word.
    3. Eliminate absolutes. All, never, only, entirely. Rarely correct unless the speaker was equally absolute.
    4. Always answer. No penalty.

    Write from Dictation highest points per second in the section

    Per-word partial credit, and it feeds Writing as well as Listening. Practice panel is on the next tab.

    1. Write the first letter of every word as you listen. Faster than whole words, and it captures the sentence length so you know whether you dropped one.
    2. Type the whole sentence even if you only recall part. Every correct word scores; there is no penalty for the ones you get wrong.
    3. Slow down on word endings. Plural -s and past-tense -ed are where the points leak, and they are the two endings a fast typist drops.
    4. Spelling is scored. A word you heard correctly and spelled wrong scores nothing, so this is the one Listening item where your spelling list earns marks directly.
    5. Ignore capitals and punctuation entirely. Neither is scored. Any second spent on them is stolen from the endings.
    6. If you cannot place a word, use grammar to position it. Word order matters, so a remembered word put in a plausible slot is worth more than one left out.
